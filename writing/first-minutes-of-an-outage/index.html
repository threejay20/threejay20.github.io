What I do in the first minutes of an outage

The first minutes decide whether an outage stays small. The aim is to restore service without creating a second problem.

November 12, 2026 · 5 min read

Everyone wants to be the engineer who fixes production. The harder skill is responding without making things worse. These are the habits I follow, and the tooling that makes them easier.

Facts before changes

I start by finding out what changed: recent deploys, configuration, permissions. Then I work out the blast radius, meaning who and what is affected. Making a change before I know either is how a small problem becomes a large one.

One change at a time

If I change three things and the service recovers, I do not know which one fixed it, and I cannot write a useful review afterwards. One change, then look at the result.

Tell people what you know

Clear updates matter as much as the fix. I came to this from the operations side: as a Senior Operations Manager at Bell Canada, I led cross-functional incident response and set clear ownership paths, which helped bring down time to resolution. In an incident, everyone knowing who owns what removes a lot of noise.

Make rollback boring

If a deploy goes wrong, getting back to a stable state should take seconds, not a meeting. In my Kubernetes lab I deploy with helm upgrade --install --atomic, which rolls back automatically if the new version never becomes ready. In an AWS static-site pipeline, rolling back means redeploying a previous commit.

Know where to look

"It was working yesterday" is usually the start of a permissions problem. When something suddenly returns access denied, I check application logs, recent deployments, configuration differences, and IAM policies and roles for a change. The fix is often five minutes. Finding it is the hard part.

Good tooling shortens the search. In my Azure lab, every Terraform change appeared in the Activity Log under the pipeline's identity. When I added a firewall rule by hand to test drift, the log showed my account made the change and when. A change by any other identity stood out immediately.

Review the system, not the person

The question after an incident is not who pushed the broken code. It is why our safety checks did not catch it before it reached production. A good review ends with a permanent guardrail: a new automated test, a stricter pipeline step, or a policy that blocks the mistake. The Azure lab's list of what I would add for production follows this pattern: Azure Policy to block public endpoints, nightly drift checks, and an alert on changes made outside the pipeline.

Systems fail. The goal is that they fail once.
