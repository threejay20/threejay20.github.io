Two findings that are not about automation

  1. Complaints and feedback is the most expensive queue at 14 minutes a ticket and $7,574 a month. Automating it would save almost nothing, because these tickets need a person. The question worth asking is why customers are raising them at all.
  2. 7% of contacts are customers asking for a human before describing a problem. That is usually a symptom of self-service that has failed them earlier, and it is a fixable experience problem rather than an automation target.

How each ticket was classified

Readable rules run first and always win. Only tickets the rules cannot match reach a language model running locally, and weak matches are held back for a person rather than guessed.

Handling a ticket and handling it correctly are different measures. Rules label most tickets, and of everything they label plus everything the model labels, 88.1% would be correct from rules alone. Adding the local model raises that to 95.8%, with 0.9% held back for a person. Measured on 5,375 tickets held back from all rule writing and example selection.

What this analysis cannot tell you

  1. The dataset is synthetic. Real customer tickets are messier, so routing accuracy on live data will be lower than shown here.
  2. Handle times and self-service targets are estimates, not measurements. They come from operational experience, and in an engagement they are replaced with the client's own figures in the first week.
  3. The split of today's self-service across contact reasons is modelled rather than measured, and it moves the saving in both directions.
  4. Savings are stated as cost avoided, not headcount removed. What an operation does with freed capacity is a management decision, not an output of this model.
